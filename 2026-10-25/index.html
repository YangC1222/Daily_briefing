English abstract

Under high-speed gas coolant conditions, wire-wrapped fuel rods in space nuclear reactors are subject to complex flow disturbances and flow-induced vibration (FIV), which may affect assembly integrity. A fluid–structure interaction (FSI) framework is developed to investigate the effects of inlet velocity (30–50 m/s) on the flow and vibration characteristics of both a single rod and a 7-pin bundle. Increasing inlet velocity enhances flow non-uniformity; for the single rod, wire-wrap induces local acceleration and wake formation, with pressure fluctuation amplitude increasing from 5.0 × 102 Pa to 1.3 × 103 Pa, while in the 7-pin bundle, wake interaction and subchannel mixing further intensify flow disturbances. Owing to the weak added-mass effect of the gas coolant, the wet modal frequencies are only slightly lower than the dry values, with nearly unchanged mode shapes, indicating negligible modification of structural dynamics. The vibration response is therefore governed by low-frequency flow excitation, with a dominant frequency of approximately 10.7 Hz consistent with the main flow fluctuation. The rod bundle exhibits strong spatial non-uniformity, where the edge rod displacement is markedly higher than that of the single rod and central rod. These results clarify the flow-induced vibration behavior governed by low-frequency flow excitation in wire-wrapped fuel rods under high-speed gas conditions and provide a basis for the safety and structural design of fuel assemblies in space gas-cooled reactor systems.

English abstract

As the key rotating component of a reactor coolant pump, the impeller of a liquid lead-bismuth eutectic (LBE) pump must withstand fluid-induced excitation and cyclic stress under high-temperature liquid-metal conditions. In this study, a one-way fluid–structure interaction framework was established for a small centrifugal pump impeller operating in LBE at 300°C. Steady and unsteady CFD simulations were performed to obtain the internal flow field and pressure pulsations. Subsequently, the stabilized transient pressure load was mapped onto a 316LN stainless steel impeller–shaft model for structural and fatigue analyses using the stress-life method and Palmgren–Miner theory. The dominant pressure pulsation frequency was 75Hz, matching the blade-passing frequency and demonstrating that fluid–structure interaction acted as the primary excitation source. Following this, the global maximum equivalent stress reached 240.76MPa near the shaft–hub/back-shroud transition, whereas the extracted blade region showed a lower peak stress of 53.48MPa. A comparison of blade-root arc paths further confirmed that the blade-root stress concentration was highly localized and varied among different blades, with a maximum path stress of 51.95MPa. Fatigue analysis revealed that the whole-model minimum-life hotspot occurred near the shaft-side shaft–hub transition, while the impeller body remained mostly in a high-life or beyond-cutoff regime. The maximum displayed fatigue damage of the impeller body was 2.073 × 10-10, and the lowest displayed life was 4.824 × 1010 repeats, indicating sufficient fatigue margin under the present loading assumption.

English abstract

The pursuit of enhanced thermal performance, improved safety margins, and compact reactor configurations in advanced nuclear systems has stimulated renewed interest in novel fuel designs. Among these concepts, helical cruciform fuel (HCF) represents a promising innovation that integrates geometric complexity with enhanced functional performance. This review synthesizes findings across thermal hydraulics, neutronics, thermo-mechanics, irradiation behavior, reactor design, and fabrication, with emphasis on evidentiary status, comparison basis, model transferability, and research needs. Geometry-driven transverse sweeping is the most consistently demonstrated HCF mechanism in single-phase bundles, but its engineering consequences are configuration-dependent. Intrinsic HCF-channel resistance can exceed that of bare cylindrical rods, whereas eliminating spacer grids can reduce nominal complete-assembly pressure loss. Increased heat-transfer area and improved mean convection can coexist with periodic local maxima in surface heat flux and wall temperature. Boiling and critical heat flux studies report both improvement and deterioration depending on flow quality, twist pitch, power distribution, geometry, and bundle scale. Modern Monte Carlo, deterministic, and coupled methods can represent HCF geometries, but reactivity, cycle length, shutdown margin, and power distribution depend strongly on fuel inventory, enrichment, moderation, absorbers, and other design choices. Thermo-mechanical evidence remains predominantly numerical and indicates material-dependent stress localization, contact loading, channel deformation, and plastic strain. Fabrication experience exists, but evidence of representative HCF-rod irradiation, commercial-scale manufacturing control, and integral assembly performance was not identified. Existing correlations and reduced-order models generally remain specific to their geometry, property range, operating regime, target quantity, and assessment basis. HCF should therefore be treated as a class of distinct candidate concepts whose benefits must be established for a specified geometry, material system, operating constraint, and reactor application.

English abstract

Fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactors (FHRs) combine tri-structural isotropic (TRISO) fuel with low-pressure liquid-salt cooling, but their pebble-bed thermal hydraulics are unusually sensitive to how the packed geometry is generated and regularized for computation. This review connects five modeling decisions—packing generation, contact-point treatment, wall-effect representation, transport-model selection, and experimental validation—to the quantities used in reactor design. Ordered simple-cubic, body-centred-cubic and face-centred-cubic beds are distinguished from confined random beds because their channel connectivity, anisotropy and wall layering arise through different mechanisms. Contact regularization is assessed by separating global geometry changes from local gap, overlap, bridge, cap and hybrid treatments. In a published structured-bed benchmark, the selected treatment changed pressure drop by about 30% and the conduction-only maximum pebble temperature by 23%; the corresponding differences fell below 0.5% for full-power thermal metrics and to 0.3% when convection was included. These values are study-specific sensitivity indicators, not universal correction factors. Evidence from pressure-drop, velocity and heat-transfer experiments is mapped to the model scale and operating range that it can support. The synthesis shows that no single contact treatment is optimal for every quantity of interest: hydraulic screening, local hot-spot prediction and passive-loss-of-forced-circulation analysis require different levels of geometric and thermal-contact fidelity. A decision framework is therefore proposed to link geometry, regularization, physics, validation and engineering use while making transferability limits explicit.
